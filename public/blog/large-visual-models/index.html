Large Visual Models Don't Exist Yet. I Want to Build Them.

August 23, 2026

What you could not do six months ago, you can do now. If you cannot do it now, you can probably do it in the next six months. I keep coming back to some version of that sentence because it keeps staying true, and I don't think it has fully sunk in for most people how strange that is. It is a crazy time to be alive and a crazy enjoyable time to be building.

Most people are using a rocket as a flashlight

Ninety-nine percent of people use AI as search. Ask a question, get an answer, close the tab. And that's fine, it's genuinely useful. But it is the smallest thing these models can do.

I know someone in his forties who is building real things and making real money, not because he learned to code at some point or picked the right startup, but because he figured out how to actually utilize AI. Not chat with it. Utilize it. Point it at work. That gap, between people who ask AI things and people who make AI do things, is quietly becoming one of the biggest divides in working life, and almost nobody is talking about it in those terms.

The models are improving fast, for more use cases, on shorter cycles than anything I've watched before. Being alive while the tools get better every quarter, and knowing how to use them, feels like a cheat code that's just sitting there.

The part nobody gets yet

Here's where my head actually is. Large language models got their moment. Language got its foundation models, and the whole world reorganized around them in about three years.

Vision hasn't had that moment. Not really, not at the level I care about. The research world has pieces of it, vision foundation models like DINOv2, papers that even use the words large vision models. But nobody owns the visual world the way language got owned. And the level I care about is fine-grained: not "this is a shoe" but "this exact shoe is real and that one is a fake," at the level of detail where money and trust change hands.

I want to build large visual models. I don't even know if that's the right name for the thing. I know what I mean by it: the LLM equivalent for seeing, and I want to be the one that makes it. MDFLD is my wedge into that problem, because authenticating football boots and kit is exactly the kind of fine-grained visual problem the current generation of models is still bad at, and I've been hand-building the dataset for it.

I was listening to David Senra interview Sam Altman this week. Sam said that in 2015, when OpenAI was founded, working on AI was the obscure, weird thing that people didn't get. That's the line that stuck. Right now people don't get MDFLD, and they don't get visual classification models, or large visual models, or whatever the name ends up being. Reading that as discouragement is one option. I read it as the tell. The thing nobody gets yet is usually the thing worth being early to. That interview put extra batteries in my back.

A 90 minute car ride

One more thing happened this week. I spent a 90 minute drive with a friend, and he spent a good part of it telling me to go to grad school. He went into debt for his. It felt like a bad decision at the time, he said so plainly. And it has paid him back in multiples since.

I wrote in my last post about hitting the ceiling of what teaching myself under pressure can get me to, and about the two boards I'm working, jobs and grad school, at the same time. His version of the argument hit different because it wasn't theory. It was a cost he actually paid and a return he actually got. There's more to say about that decision than fits here, and I'll say it in its own post soon.

What the scars say

One more thing from this week, and it's the one that ties the rest together. The message at church was about scars and healing, and it hasn't left me.

The thread went like this. After Adam and Eve fell, their first instinct was to sew fig leaves and cover themselves (Genesis 3:7). God's response was not to ignore what happened. He made them a better covering Himself (Genesis 3:21). Psalm 147:3 says He heals the brokenhearted and binds up their wounds. And when Jesus appeared after the resurrection, fully victorious, He still had the marks in His hands and the wound in His side (John 20:19-29). The scars didn't disappear. They stopped being evidence of suffering and became evidence of what He had overcome.

A wound says "I was hurt." A scar says "I was hurt, and I healed."

I have the fig leaf instinct. My default is to present things polished: ship the feature, post the win, save the messy part for later, after it has turned into a success story. The honest stuff, the company I love most getting the least of me, building from my parents' house, teaching myself everything under pressure because there was no other path, my instinct is to cover all of that until it looks like victory. Writing about the crossroads was the first time I took the fig leaves off in public, and it was uncomfortable.

But look at my friend in the car. His grad school debt was a wound once. It felt like a bad decision, he said so himself. Now it's a scar, and the scar is exactly what made his advice worth 90 minutes of my attention. Nobody learns anything from a person with no marks on them.

So I'm learning to hold my own story that way. The obscure years, the neglected company, the dataset built at night, the applications going out from a borrowed room. I don't have to hide any of it until it becomes a win. If God brings me through it, and I believe He will, these will be the scars that say healing happened here. What represents pain right now can become the testimony later. I'd rather embrace them now than pretend I never had them.

Why I'm okay doing a bunch at once

I'm doing a lot right now. Two startups, coursework, open source, applications, this blog. I'm okay with that, and honestly it's more than okay. I like being busy. I wake up every day with a bunch of things in my head and I just get right to work.

Truly, there are very few things that satisfy me or give me real joy like working on things I'm interested in. That's the whole secret. The tools got good enough that one person can act on almost everything they're interested in, if they have a system for it. The window is open. I intend to use it.
